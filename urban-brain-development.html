Urban Planning and Child Brain Development

How does exposure to traffic pollution near homes and schools relate to the developing brain?

Review at a glance

Traffic exposure at school and at home

The studies compare pollution measured in schoolyards or estimated at home addresses with MRI and motor-task results. They cover exposures from pregnancy through childhood and follow brain development into early adolescence.

Evidence across cohorts

What the studies found

Barcelona

BREATHE

Two papers drew on the same MRI subgroup of 263 children. One linked schoolyard copper to differences near the caudate. The other linked traffic-related pollution to differences in functional connectivity, a measure of how closely brain regions' activity varies together.

Rotterdam

Generation R

In 2,197 children, most of the pollution-related connections linked regions in different brain networks (20 of 26). The analysis found no significant association between road noise and the connectivity measures studied.

United States

ABCD

Three papers used overlapping samples from the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development study. Two linked air pollution to changes in brain connectivity over time. A third found that associations with white matter measures differed by sex.

Barcelona study

Schoolyard copper and motor response

Children at schools with higher copper concentrations in fine particles tended to respond more slowly and less consistently on a motor task.

Planning questions

Where are children most exposed?

School and housing locations, busy roads, and access to green space shape where children spend time. The studies report associations, so the review uses them to frame questions about which neighborhoods face the most exposure and which planning changes could reduce it.
